## Framing prompt

> Build me a 23-slide conference deck. I present it Wednesday, September 17, 2026, from 2:45 to 4:00 p.m. — the last session of a long conference day. I will apply my own Maryland SBDC design system afterward, so do not invent branding, logos, color systems, or fonts. Give me structure, layout, and typography discipline.
>
> **Audience.** About 40 established veteran-owned business owners, most with at least two years of cash flow. Mixed room: some are federal contractors, some are general small businesses. AI proficiency is unknown and spans from "never opened ChatGPT" to "pays for three tools and can't find anything." They are practitioners, not students. They have been sitting in chairs since morning. No laptops in the room; phones only.
>
> **The one idea the deck exists to deliver.** AI doesn't fix a process you can't describe. Every process is a list of steps. Each step is either Judgment (you decide) or Repetition (same thing every time). AI belongs on Repetition. The habit: write the steps, mark J or R, hand the first R to AI, keep the J.
>
> **Tone.** Direct, practical, plain language. No hype, no futurism, no "AI will transform your business." Short sentences. Dry humor is fine. Never corporate filler.
>
> **Design rules — follow these literally.**
> - One idea per slide. If a slide has two ideas, it needs to be two slides.
> - No bullet walls. Three bullets is a lot. Six is a failure.
> - Big type, readable from the back of a ballroom. Assume the last row is 60 feet out and someone is standing in front of the bottom third of the screen. Nothing smaller than a 24pt equivalent; headlines much larger.
> - Twelve of these slides are teardown slides (slides 6 through 17). Render every one of those as the filled-in worksheet layout — the same five numbered boxes as the printed handout, in the same order: 1. The process; 2–3. The steps with a J or R mark beside each (one box); 4. The first R step; 5. What I'd paste. Highlight the box or boxes that slide is about; dim the rest but keep them visible. The deck and the handout must read as the same object, so a person looking up from their paper recognizes the screen instantly.
> - On teardown slides, the worksheet content — steps, J/R tables, prompt text — is the content of the slide, not decoration. Set it legibly and do not paraphrase, trim, reword, or re-order a single line I give you.
> - Speaker notes go in the notes field, never on the slide.
>
> **Out of scope. Do not add slides for any of this:** live tool demos, tool comparisons or "which AI should I use" charts, "what is an LLM" or how models work, anything requiring a laptop, hands-on mechanics.
>
> Use exactly the 23 slides and the exact on-screen text I give you next. Do not add, merge, split, or reorder slides.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 2. Tell the story straight, nothing in your hands. A solo consultant, real credentials, good at her business, two paid subscriptions. ### Slide 3 — The thesis

**On screen:** AI doesn't fix a process you can't describe.

**Visual:** One line, largest type in the deck, centered. Nothing else.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 6. Answer the mystery: it wasn't the tools. She never wrote down what she did. She'd sit down, describe the problem fresh every time, get a good answer, and lose it. Every session started at zero. Then give them the number — roughly 90% of the owners who come to me for AI don't know what they need yet, and what they usually need isn't AI, it's a process. Land the line on the screen and stop talking for two full seconds. This is the sentence I want them repeating in the parking lot. If nothing else today lands well, this has to.

### Slide 4 — Every process is steps

**On screen:** Every process is a list of steps. Four to eight of them. If you can't write them down, AI can't help you.

**Visual:** An empty numbered list, 1 through 8, blank rules — the worksheet's box 2, unfilled.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 9. Make them concrete, because vague steps are where this dies. "Gather the data" is not a step. "Pull last week's hours from three places" is a step. Four to eight lines — if you're at twenty you've got three processes stacked on each other, split them; if you're at two you're describing a wish. Say out loud that this is the unglamorous part and the part nobody does, which is exactly why a room paying for three tools is still working Sundays. Point at the blank list and tell them they'll be filling one in about half an hour, so start deciding now which process they'd pick.

### Slide 5 — Judgment or Repetition

**On screen:** Every step is Judgment — you decide — or Repetition — same thing every time. AI goes on Repetition. You keep Judgment.

**Visual:** Two equal columns: J = Judgment, you decide. R = Repetition, same thing every time. One arrow, pointing only at the R column, labeled "AI."

**Notes:** ≈ minute 12. This is the whole session in one slide, so slow down. The test is one question per step: am I deciding something, or is this the same job every time? Deciding means J and you keep it. Same job every time means R and it's a candidate. Give them my own version, because it's the honest one: if I'm going to repeat it every Friday, I should probably automate it. Then the discipline — you don't hand over all the R steps at once. You hand over the first one you'd actually try. Say the four-move habit now and repeat it at the close: write the steps, mark J or R, hand the first R to AI, keep the J.

### Slide 6 — Teardown 1: the process and the steps

**On screen:** Teardown 1 — the floor. Build a compliance matrix from a solicitation. You could do this tonight.

**Visual:** Worksheet layout, five boxes. Boxes 1 and 2 filled and highlighted; boxes 3–5 dimmed and empty.

**Worksheet box 1 — The process:**

> Turning a solicitation's proposal instructions and evaluation criteria into a compliance matrix, before anybody writes a word of the proposal.

**Worksheet box 2 — The steps:**

> 1. Pull Section L and list every instruction on volumes, order, page counts, and format.
> 2. Pull Section M and list every evaluation factor and sub-factor.
> 3. Pull Section C or the SOW and list every "shall" statement as a separate requirement.
> 4. Line up each requirement against the Section L instruction that says where it goes and the Section M factor that says how it gets scored.
> 5. Build the outline: volume, section number, section title.
> 6. Mark each row Compliant / Intend to Comply / Partial Compliant / NON-COMPLIANT, with a risk level and a one-line justification.
> 7. Assign an owner and an action to every row that isn't already compliant.
> 8. Re-read the solicitation against the matrix and fix what you missed the first pass.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 15. Frame the block before you start: three teardowns, same five boxes every time, same order as the sheet in their hands — process, steps, J or R, first R step, what I'd paste. This first one is the floor on purpose. One prompt, one sitting, tonight. Half the room does federal work and knows what a compliance matrix is; the other half doesn't, so give them one sentence — it's the table that proves your proposal answers every requirement in the order the government asked for it. Read the eight steps fast; don't teach the RFP, teach the shape. Point out that this list existed long before I thought about AI. And name the payoff now: the output drops straight into the template we already hand out in the GovCon series.

### Slide 7 — Teardown 1: J or R

**On screen:** Five steps are Repetition. Three are Judgment. The Judgment ones are where you bet your bid.

**Visual:** Worksheet layout, box 3 highlighted. J rows in a much heavier weight than R rows so the pattern reads from the back row.

**Worksheet box 3 — J or R:**

| Step | J/R | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. List Section L instructions | R | Transcription. Same job on every solicitation. |
| 2. List Section M factors | R | Transcription. |
| 3. Pull every "shall" as a requirement | R | Pattern-matching against a rulebook, not a call. |
| 4. Map requirement → L instruction → M factor | R | Mechanical once all three lists exist. |
| 5. Build the outline | R | Section L already dictated it. |
| 6. Compliance status, risk, justification | **J** | This is you betting your bid. Nobody else. |
| 7. Assign owner and action | **J** | You know who can actually write it by Thursday. |
| 8. Second pass against the matrix | **J** | Judgment is the whole point of a review. |

**Notes:** ≈ minute 17. Walk the column, not the table. Steps 1 through 5 are transcription and mapping — the same job on every solicitation, which is the definition of R. Then it flips hard at step 6. Marking a row Compliant is you betting your bid, and nobody else can do that for you. Step 7 is judgment because you know who on your team can actually write it by Thursday. Step 8 is judgment because judgment is the whole point of a review. Then say the uncomfortable part: most people run this backwards. They let the tool grade their compliance and they do the transcription by hand. That's exactly inverted, and it's the most expensive way to use AI on a bid.

### Slide 8 — Teardown 1: first R step and what I'd paste

**On screen:** First R step: step 3 — pull every "shall." Because you can check it yourself in a minute.

**Visual:** Worksheet layout, boxes 4 and 5 highlighted together. Prompt set as a monospaced block; the brackets visually distinct.

**Worksheet box 4 — The first R step:**

> Step 3 — pulling every "shall" out of the SOW. Not because it's the biggest. Because it's the one you're most likely to do badly at 11 p.m. and the one where missing a single line gets you thrown out on compliance before anyone reads your technical approach. It's also self-checking: you can spot-count the "shall" statements yourself and see whether the list is complete.

**Worksheet box 5 — What I'd paste:**

> Here is [Sections L, M, and C of a solicitation, pasted]. Turn it into [a proposal compliance matrix] in this format: [Outline # | Proposal Section Title | Section in Proposal | RFP Section L | Section # | RFP Section M | Section # | RFP Section C/SOW | Compliance Status | Risk Level | Justification | Action | Owner]. One row per requirement. Leave Compliance Status, Risk Level, Justification, Action, and Owner blank — I fill those in. Flag anything that looks off [conflicting page limits, a Section M factor with no matching Section L instruction, a "shall" you couldn't place anywhere].

**Notes:** ≈ minute 19. Two things to point at. First, why step 3 and not step 1: because it's self-checking. You can spot-count the "shall" statements yourself and know in a minute whether the list is complete. Pick a first R step you can verify — that rule is on their sheet. Second, point at the sentence telling it to leave those columns blank. That's me refusing to hand over the J steps inside a prompt that's doing R work. Then name the skeleton, because it repeats in all three teardowns: here is what I have, turn it into what I need, in this format, flag anything that looks off. Four moves. That's the entire prompt-writing curriculum for today.

### Slide 9 — Teardown 1: what went sideways

**On screen:** It filled in my Compliance Status column. Every row said "Compliant." It had no idea.

**Visual:** Same worksheet frame, all five boxes dimmed, one callout panel over the top labeled "What went sideways."

**Notes:** ≈ minute 22. Be honest here, it buys you the room. The first version I ran filled in the Compliance Status column for me and every single row said "Compliant." It had no idea whether we could actually do any of it — it was pattern-matching on what a finished matrix looks like. That's why the prompt now says leave those columns blank. The second failure was quieter and worse: it silently merged compound requirements. One "shall" with three clauses came back as one row, so three obligations shared one owner and one status. Now I ask it to split compound requirements into separate rows and I still eyeball the count myself. The lesson behind them on the screen: confident output is not correct output.

### Slide 10 — Teardown 2: the process and the steps

**On screen:** Teardown 2 — the middle. Chasing a past-due invoice without setting the relationship on fire.

**Visual:** Worksheet layout, boxes 1 and 2 filled and highlighted; boxes 3–5 dimmed.

**Worksheet box 1 — The process:**

> Following up on an invoice that's past due, without setting the relationship on fire.

**Worksheet box 2 — The steps:**

> 1. Pull the open invoices and the aging from the accounting file.
> 2. Pull the last email thread with that customer so you know what was already said and when.
> 3. Check what else is in play with them — open work, a pending change order, a renewal.
> 4. Draft the nudge.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 24. This is the one everybody in the room has. Ask it plainly: who's got an invoice out past 30 days right now? Say why it's the middle teardown — the first one was repetition with the judgment at the end; this one has a judgment step buried in the middle, and that's where people get hurt. Then name the real problem before the steps: the reason an invoice is 45 days old is almost never that you didn't know it was late. It's that you didn't want to write the email. Reading the steps, flag step 3 as the one most people skip and step 5 as the one worth more than the other six combined.

**Worksheet box 3 — J or R:**

| Step | J/R | Why |

| 1. Pull open invoices and aging | R | The report is the report. |
| 2. Pull the last thread | R | Retrieval. |
| 3. Check what else is in play | R | Look-up. You're gathering facts, not weighing them. |

### Slide 12 — Teardown 2: first R step and what I'd paste

**On screen:** First R step: step 4 — draft the nudge. Ask for three tones. You still pick.

**Visual:** Worksheet layout, boxes 4 and 5 highlighted. Prompt monospaced; "Give me three versions" set slightly heavier.

**Worksheet box 4 — The first R step:**

> Step 4 — draft the nudge. It's the step you avoid, which is why the invoice is 45 days old instead of 20. Handing the draft to AI removes the reason you procrastinate: staring at a blank reply box trying to sound firm and friendly at the same time. You keep step 5 completely. The draft shows up, you read it, you decide whether this is a soft reminder to a good customer or the last email before you stop work.

**Worksheet box 5 — What I'd paste:**

> Here is [the invoice details: number, amount, date sent, days past due, and the last email thread with this customer]. Turn it into [a short follow-up email, four sentences maximum, in the tone I mark below] in this format: [one line of context, one line stating amount and original due date, one specific ask with a date, one line offering to talk]. Give me three versions: friendly reminder, firm, and final notice before we pause work. Flag anything that looks off [I already said something contradictory in the thread, the amount doesn't match what was quoted, there's an open dispute I'd be stepping on].

**Notes:** ≈ minute 28. The move here is asking for three versions, and say out loud why. You're not asking AI to pick the tone. You're asking it to lay out the options so your judgment call takes ten seconds instead of ten avoided days. That's the whole trick — you didn't automate the decision, you removed the friction sitting in front of it. Point at the "flag anything that looks off" clause too, because in this one it earns its keep: it catches the case where you already said something contradictory in the thread, or the amount doesn't match what you quoted. And note it's the same four-move skeleton as teardown one. Not a different prompt — the same prompt with different blanks.

### Slide 13 — Teardown 2: what went sideways

**On screen:** A fully automated collections process called her customers. She kept none of them.

**Visual:** Worksheet frame dimmed, "What went sideways" callout over the top, with a second smaller panel for the quiet version. No stock imagery.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 30. Two failures, both on the judgment step. Tell the first one flat, no editorializing: an owner in a cash crunch signed with an offshore working-capital lender. Her customer emails were fluent, warm, and completely generic — my read is her repeat customers could tell.

### Slide 14 — Teardown 3: the process and the steps

**On screen:** Teardown 3 — the ceiling. Friday 4 p.m.: three systems in, one report out, on a schedule.

**Visual:** Worksheet layout, boxes 1 and 2 filled and highlighted; boxes 3–5 dimmed.

**Worksheet box 1 — The process:**

> Every Friday at 4 p.m., a scheduled job reads my week out of three systems and writes an entry to my action log so I stop losing track of my own open loops.

**Worksheet box 2 — The steps:**

> 1. Set the date range: Monday through today.
> 2. Pull the past seven days of email — sent and received — and note commitments, follow-ups, and decisions.
> 3. Pull the calendar for the week: who I met, what type of meeting, anything recurring.
> 4. Read my own AI session transcripts for the week: what got built, what changed, what got decided.
> 5. Write the entry in a fixed six-part shape: Synthesis, client meetings by day, email activity, sessions, personal, open loops.
> 6. Append it to the action log. Never overwrite — read the file, add to the bottom, write it back.
> 7. I read it Friday evening and correct it.

**Notes:** ≈ minute 32. This one is mine, and I'm showing it so they see where this goes — not so anybody builds it this month. Say that out loud early or half the room will start planning a scheduled job tonight. Three sources in: email, calendar, my own session transcripts. One entry out, in the same six-part shape every week. Point at step 5 and say the fixed shape is the only reason it works at all — the week it becomes freeform is the week I stop reading it. Point at step 6, append and never overwrite, and say the rule is in writing because I learned it the hard way. Then set up the next slide: this did not start as a scheduled job.

### Slide 16 — Teardown 3: first R step and what I'd paste

**On screen:** It started as step 3 — the calendar pull. One clean source I could check in 30 seconds.

**Worksheet box 4 — The first R step:**

> Step 3, the calendar pull. Smallest possible piece, one clean source, and I could check it in 30 seconds. That's where this started — not with the scheduled job. The scheduled job came later, once the manual version was boring.

**Worksheet box 5 — What I'd paste:**

> Here is [my calendar for this week and the past seven days of email]. Turn it into [a weekly action log entry] in this format: [Synthesis — one paragraph, three to five sentences; Client Meetings — one line per meeting grouped by day as Day: Name / Company — meeting type (brief context); Email Activity — one line each, skip routine; Sessions — one line each; Personal — one line each, skip if empty; Open Loops — one line each, things carrying into next week].
