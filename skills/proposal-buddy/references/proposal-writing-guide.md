# Proposal Writing Guide

Distilled from the Maryland SBDC "Winning That Government Contracting Award" workshop series (Sessions 1-4) and the Shipley POWeR methodology.

---

## The Starting Point

Three common instincts when facing a blank page — all wrong:

1. **Pull from a previous proposal** — it was written for a different customer, RFP, and criteria. Evaluators can tell when content doesn't fit.
2. **Grab marketing materials** — marketing copy is written to impress, not to score against evaluation criteria.
3. **Write a technical manual** — evaluators aren't reading to learn. They're reading to score.

The right starting point: pull up your compliance matrix. What requirement are you addressing? What does Section L say? Now you know exactly what to write and how it'll be scored.

---

## The Three C's: Compliant → Correct → Compelling

| Level | What It Means | What Happens If You Miss It |
|-------|--------------|----------------------------|
| Compliant | Followed all Section L instructions; addressed every requirement | Proposal can be eliminated before evaluation |
| Correct | Solution is technically sound; numbers add up; claims are accurate | Evaluators rate you "Acceptable" — in the game, not winning |
| Compelling | Clear win themes, quantified benefits, customer focus | Evaluators rate you "Outstanding" or "Good" — you win |

Most small businesses reach Compliant and Correct. The gap is almost always Compelling.

## Features vs. Benefits

The backbone of persuasive proposal writing. Use the "So What?" test after every sentence.

**Feature:** A characteristic of your solution — what you have.
**Benefit:** The advantage the customer gets — what it does for them.

### Progression: Weak → Strong

**Weak — Feature Only:**
"Our company has provided help desk support for 10 years."

**Better — Feature + Benefit:**
"Our 10 years of help desk support enables us to resolve 85% of tickets at Tier 1, reducing escalations and improving end-user satisfaction."

**Best — Feature + Benefit + Proof + Customer Focus:**
"Drawing on 10 years across three DoD agencies, our team resolves 85% of tickets at Tier 1 — exceeding the Army's 80% target and reducing escalation volume by 30%. This means faster resolution for Fort Meade's 12,000 end users."

**Formula:** Feature → Quantified Benefit → Proof → Customer Impact. Use this for every key claim.

## Win Themes

What you want the evaluator to remember about your company.

### What makes a strong win theme:
- Provides the conclusion you want the evaluator to reach
- Includes a quantified feature and a customer benefit
- Puts the customer first
- Uses the RFP's own language
- No more than 2-3 sentences (40 words max)
- Keep to 3-4 themes maximum

### Theme progression:

**Good:** "Our on-board computer is fully responsive to the RFP requirements."
(Compliance only — doesn't differentiate)

**Better:** "Our proposed on-board computer exceeds all specified performance requirements and is only 50 percent of the allowed weight and size."
(Adds specificity and quantification)

**Best:** "Meeting NASA's goals for top performance with minimized equipment load, our proposed on-board computer exceeds all specified performance requirements and is only 50 percent of the allowed weight and size."
(Leads with the customer's goal)

**Key insight:** Themes aren't repeated word-for-word throughout the proposal — they're ideas whose essence runs through everything. Each section reinforces the theme from a different angle.

## Discriminators

What makes you different in a way that matters.

**Counts as a discriminator:**
- Proprietary methodology with documented results
- Specific past performance on a nearly identical program
- Key personnel the customer already knows and trusts
- Local presence when competitors are remote
- A certification or clearance competitors lack

**Doesn't count:**
- "We have the best people" — everyone says this
- "We're committed to quality" — prove it or cut it
- Generic technology claims without specifics

A discriminator must address something the customer cares about. Being different isn't enough — being different in a way that matters is.

## Ghosting

Subtle competitive positioning. Highlight your strength where a competitor is likely weak — without naming them.

**How it works:** Identify competitor's probable weakness → offer your alternative as superior → let the evaluator draw their own conclusion.

**Example:** You know the incumbent has had delivery delays. You write:
"Our proven scheduling methodology has delivered 100% of milestones on time across three comparable contracts, ensuring [Agency] maintains uninterrupted operations during the transition period."

Never name competitors. Never insult. The evaluator is smart enough to see the contrast.

## Customer Focus

### The 2:1 Rule
Customer name appears roughly twice as often as your company name.

**Before:** "Acme Solutions has 15 years of experience in logistics management."
**After:** "Fort Meade's logistics operations will benefit from our 15 years of experience managing comparable programs across three DoD installations."

Same information. Completely different focus.

### Name the customer before your company in every sentence
"Fort Meade's operations will benefit from our..." not "Our company will provide Fort Meade with..."

## Writing Mechanics

### Do:
- Address every requirement — be compliant and responsive
- Use the RFP's exact language ("plan" not "approach")
- Respond in the same order as the RFP
- Use clear headings and strong topic sentences
- Explicitly connect responses to requirements ("In response to Section C, 3.7...")
- Write simple, declarative sentences — subject, verb, object
- Quantify everything — numbers are specific, verifiable, and scoreable
- Back up every claim with evidence
- Use active voice — "Our team will deliver" not "Deliverables will be provided by"
- Keep paragraphs to 4-5 sentences maximum
- BLUF every paragraph — lead with your key point

### Don't:
- Rewrite requirements in your own words
- Reorganize into what you think is a more logical order
- Make "leaps in logic" — explain every step
- Bury best content in the middle of a paragraph

**Test:** If the evaluator only reads the first sentence of each paragraph, they should still get the main message.

## Words and Phrases That Kill Your Score

### Cut immediately:
- "World-class" / "best-of-breed" / "state-of-the-art" / "cutting-edge"
- "We are pleased to..." / "We believe..." / "We intend to..." / "We will strive to..."
- "We understand..." — prove it, don't claim it
- "We are the only company that can..." — if you were, you'd already have the contract

### Replace with:
- Specific, quantified claims backed by evidence
- Direct statements: "Our team will..." not "We believe our team can..."
- Demonstrated understanding through your approach — not a statement of understanding

**Rule of thumb:** If a sentence would be true for any company bidding on this contract, it's not worth writing.

## Graphics

### Types to include:
- **Process diagrams** — methodology step by step with inputs, outputs, decision points
- **Organizational charts** — team structure with actual proposed staff names and certifications
- **Schedule graphics** — actual RFP milestones with proposed completion dates
- **Comparison tables** — validate claims with data

### Rules:
- Every graphic needs a title, figure number, and action caption
- The caption reinforces a win theme
- The graphic should stand alone — if evaluator only looked at graphics, they should understand your approach
- No clip art — everything original and specific to this proposal

**Example caption:**
Not: "Figure 3. Org Chart."
Instead: "Figure 3. Program Management Organization. Our flat reporting structure enables rapid decision-making, reducing issue resolution time by 40%."

## The Color Review Process

### Pink Team (~30-40% complete)
Reviews strategy, approach, and compliance direction. Early enough for major changes.
Focus: "Are we answering the right questions? Are our themes showing up? Are we missing any requirements?"

### Red Team (~80-90% complete)
Simulated government evaluation. Reviewers score using actual Section M criteria.
Focus: Strengths, weaknesses, and deficiencies — in government evaluation language.

### Gold Team (final)
Executive review. "Would I buy from this company?"

**Small business version:** Find 2-3 trusted reviewers — a former contracting officer, a teaming partner, a fellow small business owner. Even one outside reviewer catches what you'll miss after three weeks of staring at the same document.

## Common Proposal Writing Mistakes

1. **Misreading the RFP** — compliance matrix prevents this
2. **All about you** — focusing on capabilities instead of customer benefits
3. **Telling, not showing** — describing what you'll do without explaining how
4. **No differentiation** — writing a proposal any competitor could have submitted
5. **Unsubstantiated claims** — "We're the best" without evidence
6. **Ignoring graphics** — walls of text with no visual communication
7. **Writing before building the matrix** — guaranteed to miss requirements and waste time

## The POWeR Schedule

- **25% Planning and Organizing** (first week of a 30-day cycle)
- **50% Writing** (weeks 2-3)
- **25% Examining and Revising** (final week)

"If you're still writing the night before it's due, you skipped the examination step — and your score will show it."
